PREFACE

What is this project, in one minute?

A modular, upgradable RC-style rover: a small four-wheel car that we build in stages, starting as a basic remote-controlled vehicle and growing from there.

  • Its main idea: the battery is a removable cartridge. Instead of stopping to recharge, the rover swaps in a fresh one and a station charges the old one.
  • How it stays cheap: a few motors are shared between driving and the swap mechanisms, within a student budget of about PKR 25,000.
  • Where it goes next: sensors, then remote control over Wi-Fi, then autonomous driving, and further upgrades if time allows.
  • Why it matters: it is a small-scale version of what NIO and Lahore's Zyp and NeuBolt do for cars, bikes and rickshaws.

    BACKGROUND

    Mobile robots meet the EV energy problem

    • Area: small electric mobile robots and the way they store and replace energy.
    • Why it matters: every robot or EV that recharges in place is out of service while it charges.
    • Existing technology: NIO's automated car battery swap stations; in Lahore, Zyp (motorcycles) and NeuBolt (rickshaws) do swapping for bikes and three-wheelers.
    • Our motivation: test whether a small, low-cost rover can do the swap itself, as a hands-on learning project.

    (Diagram: swap-station concept sketch to be shown on CAD.)

    THE PROBLEM

    Fixed batteries force a trade-off small rovers shouldn't have to make

    • A fixed pack means every recharge is downtime: hours out of service.
    • Sizing for long runtime means carrying dead weight; sizing small means constant stops.
    • One onboard pack is a single point of failure.
    • Our goal: show, on a student budget, the principle full-size EVs use: make the energy store modular and swappable.
    PROBLEM STATEMENT & AIM

    Problem statement and aim

    The existing small rovers with fixed batteries have the limitation of long recharge downtime and a single pack as a point of failure, resulting in interrupted operation and oversized, heavy packs. Therefore, there is a need to develop a rover that swaps its own battery cartridges at a charging station.

    Aim

    To design and build a low-cost modular rover that exchanges its battery cartridges automatically, showing hot-swappable energy storage at small scale.

    OBJECTIVES

    Objectives

    • 1. Build a 4WD rover on a 300 × 200 mm chassis.
    • 2. Design a removable battery cartridge and a swap mechanism with shared motors.
    • 3. Build a passive charging station with a self-aligning dock.
    • 4. Add sensors, remote control and, if possible, autonomous docking.
    • 5. Complete the project within PKR 25,000 ± 3,000 in about 4 months.
    THE CONCEPT

    One rover, five motors, nine jobs

    When the rover stops to swap, its four drive motors sit idle. A 5th motor slides all four backward, disengaging them from the wheels, and hands their power to swap mechanisms. Spring preload brakes the wheels automatically during the same slide.

    Drive (4 jobs)

    4WD, gear-coupled wheels, sliding motor mounts. With an H-bridge between the motors and the Arduino, we can drive them in both directions.

    Clamp

    Worm-gear reduction: self-locking, holds the cartridge with zero standing current.

    Carousel

    Two positions only (left / right), spring detents, microswitch confirms seating.

    Insertion + station turn

    Screw-push plus cam-drop; external gear turns the station carousel.

    Camera/sensor/antena rotation (9th job)

    The same shared drive rotates either the camera or surrounding sensors/antenas, to be tested and whichever is best will be used.

    WORKING PRINCIPLE

    Input → Process → Output

    Input

    Battery low (voltage, current, temperature) or manual trigger

    →

    Dock

    Rover drives to the station; funnel and bump align it

    →

    Disengage

    5th motor slides drive motors back; springs brake wheels

    →

    Swap

    Clamp, carousel and insertion exchange the cartridge

    →

    Output

    Fresh battery live; rover resumes driving

    Development stages

    • CAD in Onshape, then 3D-printing and assembly, then Arduino/ESP32 control, then testing.
    DRIVETRAIN

    Drivetrain: preliminary sizing, work in progress

    We are only in week 1. Every number below is a first estimate to be revised as parts are chosen, weighed and tested.

    • Chassis 300 × 200 mm, wheels 80 mm, target mass 2–4 kg.
    • Rough worst case (assumed 4 kg, ~15° docking ramp): on the order of 1 kgf·cm per wheel. Real mass and ramp angle are still unknown.
    • Current leaning: TT-style gearmotors around 1:100–1:150, plastic gearbox (short duty cycle). Not final.
    • Starting layout: track ≈165 mm, wheelbase ≈150 mm, so near 1:1 to keep skid-steer turning scrub low. May change with the real motor and wheel dimensions.
    • All four motors identical: a DC motor already trades speed for torque under load.
    • Planned: stall guard in software (cut PWM if current stays high for 1–2 s).
    • Status: work in progress. Torque, speed, mass and layout will be refined by testing.
    PRELIMINARY SPECIFICATIONS

    Specs (preliminary, will be refined)

    • Dimensions: chassis 300 × 200 mm; wheels 80 mm.
    • Material: mostly plastic / 3D-printed parts.
    • Power: 2S Li-ion cartridges, ~7.4 V, ~2,500 mAh each; 5 V logic via buck converter.
    • Capacity: 2 cartridges, estimated ~1.4 h each.
    • Operating range: indoor flat floor; teleoperation range depends on Wi-Fi (to be tested).
    • Control: ESP32 with H-bridge motor drivers.
    • Major components: 5 gearmotors, cartridges, station, sensors, cameras.
    • Target performance: total mass 2–4 kg; swap about 20 s.
    SWAP MECHANISM

    Mostly electrical, with one small moving shaft

    • The swap is primarily an electrical handover between two cartridges (next slide).
    • The mechanical part is simple: one shaft that shifts slightly sideways, carrying 2–3 gears (one input, two outputs).
    • Shifting the shaft selects which output the single motor drives, so one motor does two jobs with no clutch complexity.
    • Carousel: left/right detents. Clamp: worm gear. Insertion: screw stroke then cam-drop.
    • Swap takes about 20 seconds; demo can trigger it manually rather than waiting for depletion.
    POWER & SAFETY

    Seamless handover, designed against failure cases

    • Two cartridges, each 2 × 18650 (2S, ~2,500 mAh) with its own 2S protection board and thermistor, in a rigid housing.
    • Diode-OR handover: contacts are spaced so only 1–2 lines connect at once; the higher-voltage pack supplies the load, with no cross-current.
    • One buck converter gives the ESP32 a steady 5 V, so voltage dips during the swap never reach it.
    • TB6612 motor drivers (lower drop than L298N) straight off the raw rail.
    • Runtime ≈ 1.4 h per cartridge, so ~2.9 h across both, before recharge.
    STATION & DOCKING

    A passive station: the rover does the work

    • Station has no motor: the rover's reassigned motor turns its 2-slot carousel through a gear mesh.
    • Each slot has its own always-on CC-CV charge module (TP5100-class); charging stops automatically at full.
    • Self-aligning dock: funnel walls fix left/right and heading; a bump-and-dip under the rear wheels fixes forward/back. Geometry alone, no precision sensing.
    • Cameras and teleoperation handle the coarse approach; geometry finishes the last few millimetres.
    • Alternative, as NIO does it: with a somewhat higher budget, the station could do most of the battery changing, holding the motors and mechanisms itself. That makes the rover lighter and simpler, at the cost of a more expensive station.

    REAL-WORLD PRECEDENT

    NIO does this with cars

    NIO is a Chinese electric-car company that built a network of Power Swap Stations: a car drives in and its whole battery is exchanged automatically in a few minutes, instead of waiting to charge.

    • Drivers never wait for charging; the company manages and charges packs centrally.
    • The principle is scale-independent: it works whether the vehicle weighs 2 kg or 2 tonnes.
    • NIO swaps from below with a large robotic platform; ours is a horizontal, motor-shared carousel, deliberately leaner for a student budget.
    • Our contribution: showing swapping without a dedicated actuator for every function.
    BATTERY SWAPPING FOR BIKES

    The e-bike strategy, and Lahore's own examples

    The strategy

    • Rider owns (or rents) the bike but not the battery; batteries are exchanged at a station in about a minute.
    • Upfront bike cost drops, range anxiety disappears, and the operator charges packs cheaply and at scale (even from solar).

    In Lahore

    • Zyp Technologies (Lahore; CEO Hassan Khan): electric motorcycles plus battery swap stations. Raised a $1.2M seed (2023) and $1.5M pre-Series A (2024). Reported status: active, scaling local assembly and rolling out its swap network for commercial riders and delivery fleets.
    • NeuBolt (LUMS spin-off, founded by Dr. Naveed Arshad): swap stations for electric rickshaws (three-wheelers). Reported status: active, research-backed, running commercial trials and expanding across Punjab; a LUMS-led project targets 137 stations.
    • Our project is the same idea, miniaturised and automated.
    WHY IT SCALES

    A moon-base rover makes the same argument

    If a rover shuttles within a small, repeatedly visited area, it is cheaper to put the solar panels and charger at one spot than on the rover itself.

    • Every kilogram launched to the Moon is expensive; a rover that carries only swappable batteries is lighter.
    • One station can serve several light rovers instead of each hauling charging hardware it barely uses.
    • This mirrors our design: heavy, stationary hardware at the station; the mobile unit stays simple.
    ECONOMICS

    Where the money is saved, and where it isn't

    • Shared motors: reusing the 4 drive motors as swap actuators avoids buying separate motors for several jobs, roughly PKR 2–3k saved.
    • Passive station: charge modules stay fixed; the rover carries no charger.
    • Batteries are the big win at fleet scale: one charged spare serves many vehicles, so fewer cells sit idle.
    • Honest cost: more gears and precision printing, and more single points of failure. We accept that trade knowingly.
    • Battery-as-a-service (the Zyp/NIO model) turns a large upfront battery cost into a small per-swap fee.

    POSSIBLE QUESTIONS 1/3

    Concept and design

    Why swap instead of fitting a bigger battery?
    Runtime versus downtime and weight; smaller packs are easier to protect, and it demonstrates swap technology.
    Isn't sharing motors just extra complexity?
    It saves cost but concentrates risk. We compared both and chose it knowingly; the design is staged so the swap is proven first.
    How does the swap actually work?
    Mostly an electrical handover between two cartridges, plus one shaft that shifts slightly sideways to select between two gear outputs.
    Why sliding motors instead of lifting?
    A small sideways slide gives repeatable gear disengagement and lets a spring apply the brake at the same time.
    Why 4WD skid-steer?
    No steering linkage, cheaper; track and wheelbase were tuned to reduce turning scrub.
    Why plastic gears?
    Demo duty cycle is tiny compared to rated life; torque is set by ratio, not material.
    POSSIBLE QUESTIONS 2/3

    Power, safety and reliability

    What if the swap jams mid-cycle?
    Detents, self-locking worm gear and diode-OR limit the damage; the shared mechanism is our highest-risk part and we say so.
    How do you protect the lithium cells?
    A CC-CV charge module for normal charging, plus a protection board in each cartridge as backup.
    What happens if contacts touch at the wrong time?
    Contacts are spaced so only one or two lines connect at once; diodes block backflow between packs.
    Why doesn't the ESP32 reset during the swap?
    The ESP32 is the rover's "brain": a tiny, cheap Wi-Fi-enabled computer chip that reads sensors and controls the motors. It runs from a buck converter, which absorbs the rail's dips and jumps.
    How do you know when to swap?
    State of charge estimated from voltage, current and temperature together.
    What if the batteries wear out?
    Li-ion lasts hundreds of cycles, far beyond demo use; cartridges are replaceable by design.
    POSSIBLE QUESTIONS 3/3

    Practicality, cost and future

    What is the biggest technical risk?
    Docking alignment and torque sizing under real load.
    How does this compare to NIO or Zyp?
    Same principle at a much smaller scale and budget; we test the mechanism idea, not their business scale.
    Is this commercially viable?
    As a rover, it is a proof of concept. The value is in the mechanism and the battery-as-a-service model.
    What if you go over budget?
    The phases are cut points: swap first, sensors next, and autonomy only if funds remain.
    What will you show if autonomy isn't ready?
    A teleoperated demo with a manual swap trigger still proves the core idea.
    What did you learn or change during design?
    Motor sizing, the diode handover and the passive station all changed after calculation.
